European Design Festival, Sofia · Teaser · 2026

IN THE SKIRTS OF VITOSHA

Vitosha is the mountain Sofia sits under. In Bulgarian a mountain’s foothills are its poli — its skirts — and they are where the city ends and the mountain starts. In 2026 the European Design Festival came to Sofia, and this is the site that announced it: a strip of woven textile that, as you swipe, rises into the outline of Vitosha.

It works on a phone, held sideways, and nowhere else.

Role
Front-end and WebGL development. Fabric shaders, scroll and pacing system, device gates, and the full site build.

The decision to refuse

Open it on a laptop and it tells you to open it on a phone. Open it on a phone held upright and it tells you to turn it. There is no fallback, no reduced desktop version, no “continue anyway” link.

That is a lot of traffic turned away on purpose, on a site whose entire job is announcing a festival to an international design audience — most of whom will first see the link on a desktop, in an email or on Slack.

It was a team decision, made on purpose. A teaser has one job — to make you feel something for thirty seconds — and a panorama you drag with your thumb only makes sense on a phone held sideways.

So the gate had to be strict, and cheap. Plain CSS decides which of the three screens you get — desktop, rotate, or the experience — from screen size, touch versus mouse, and orientation. In portrait every scroll and every animation is frozen, so nothing moves behind the rotate message, and when you turn the phone back you carry on exactly where you were.

Why landscape is harder than it sounds

You cannot reliably lock a phone’s orientation from a website. Android allows the Screen Orientation API’s lock(), but only in fullscreen; iOS Safari ignores it entirely. So the site asks for fullscreen and a landscape lock on the very first tap — the only moment browsers allow it — trying several elements in turn, because different mobile browsers accept it on different ones. Where that fails, the only move left is to detect, ask, and wait, which makes the gate a real piece of UI rather than a one-line check.

And landscape on a phone is the most hostile viewport on the web:

  • Vertical space collapses once the browser chrome is accounted for.
  • The address bar shows and hides as you interact, and rotating the phone resizes the page too. Both looked like a scroll and could start the sequence by itself. It now starts only after a real gesture — swipe, wheel or key — and resize-driven movement in the first 0.6 seconds is ignored.
  • Notches and home indicators eat the edges — hence viewport-fit=cover and the safe-area insets.
  • iPhone users with rotation lock on have to open Control Center to turn the phone. On iOS that doesn’t hide the page, so the animation kept running underneath. The whole experience now pauses whenever the window loses focus, the tab is hidden, or Safari freezes the page, and resumes where it stopped.
  • The theme colours were set by the main script, so text flashed in the wrong colour while it downloaded. A tiny inline script in the head now picks the textile and sets the colours before anything is painted, then hands the choice to the main code.

The site also ships the Apple web-app meta tags with a translucent status bar, so added to the home screen it runs full-bleed with no browser furniture at all.

The experience itself

Before you touch it, the cloth sways as if in a breeze — stronger at the hem, still at the sides, like fabric pinned at both ends. The first swipe fades the title and starts the piece. Swipe sideways or up and down, both work, and the fabric slides along while its top edge slowly rises from flat into the ridge of Vitosha.

As it moves, peaks light up along the ridge — Kopitoto, Aleko, Cherni Vrah — each paired with the neighbourhoods at its foot, Boyana, Dragalevtsi, Simeonovo, and each pair arrives with one big word for what the festival offers: design, exhibitions, talks, studio visits, workshops, awards, party. After the last pair the scrolling stops, the mountain surges higher, the fabric starts to shimmer, and only the two real venues stay on screen, Cinema Lumière and Toplocentrala, marked as stars. Then the end screen: Share, and Restart, which reloads with a different textile.

A mountain drawn out of a drawing

The designer drew the outline of Vitosha as an SVG — one line for the ridge, one for the foothills. On load the site draws that SVG onto an invisible canvas and, for every column of pixels, finds where the line sits. The result is a heightmap: a bar chart of the mountain’s profile. The two ends are blended together so the panorama loops without a seam, and a very light smoothing takes out the pixel stair-steps while keeping the sharp, angular peaks the designer wanted.

The fabric itself is a Three.js plane cut into a 384 × 96 grid. A vertex shader pushes every point of it up or down from the heightmap — the top edge follows the ridge, the bottom follows the foothills — by an amount tied to how far you’ve scrolled. A second shader does the melting: it drags the textile’s pixels along the shape of the mountain and pulls the red, green and blue channels slightly apart. On the right of the screen there’s a clean zone where the effect fades out so the cloth reads calmly. Each textile has its own tuned settings, which is why one is all rainbow edges and another barely melts at all.

The textile photos came with torn, transparent top edges, and stretched upward they left a dark outline around the mountain. On load, each column now finds its first solid pixel of fabric and copies that colour upward. On the third textile the white stitching along the torn edge is kept on purpose, at the art director’s request.

The peak and place names aren’t part of the 3D image. They’re ordinary HTML — sharp, and easy to style — and every frame the code works out where the mountain line is on screen and moves each label onto it, so they look stitched to the moving cloth.

A speed limit on a swipe

The first version let a quick flick fly past three or four pairs, and people missed the words — which were the whole message. So the panorama got a speed limit. A swipe sets a target, and the fabric glides towards it at a capped speed. The leftover glide is capped too, so swiping harder never builds up a long runaway slide.

The real peaks aren’t evenly spaced, so tying the words to them made the rhythm lurch. The reveals are paced instead in equal steps of scroll, based on the average distance between peaks, so the words land at a steady beat while each pair is still on screen. Every word gets about 1.2 seconds, and it still feels like your finger is moving it, not like a video playing.

The words themselves appear in 0.4 seconds, hold for 0.15, and melt away over 1.1 — timings tuned with the art director on real phones. Some letters are set in italic, and which ones is controlled by writing them in uppercase in the word list — dEsiGn, wOrksHops — which made them easy to adjust together with the designer.

Outcome

The festival ran 11–14 June 2026 in Sofia, at Cinema Lumière and Toplocentrala — the two stars left on the mountain at the end.
